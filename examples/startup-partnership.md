# Example: Startup Partnership With Partially Aligned Incentives

## User prompt

> A potential cofounder has resources and connections that I need. We agree on the short-term goal, but I am not sure our long-term incentives are aligned. If I wait, I may lose the opportunity. Should I partner now?

## Decision-structure kernel

- **Goal:** gain complementary resources without giving away too much future control.
- **Power:** each side possesses a resource the other wants, but dependence may become asymmetric after commitment.
- **Information asymmetry:** true long-term intentions are difficult to verify before working together.
- **Time pressure:** waiting has opportunity cost.
- **Reversibility:** equity, control rights, public commitments, and shared IP can be difficult to unwind.
- **Tail risk:** early convenience creates structural dependence or a future governance deadlock.

## Historical retrieval strategy

Look for cases involving:

- alliances formed because each side temporarily needs the other;
- coalition partners whose long-term interests diverge;
- granting power or resources before loyalty is proven;
- staged cooperation, hostages, guarantees, offices, territory, or other mechanisms used to manage trust.

Do not equate ancient political alliances with startup contracts. The useful analogy is the **incentive and dependency structure**, not the institution.

## Questions the historical cases should help answer

- Did the actor reduce uncertainty before making the relationship irreversible?
- Was cooperation staged, or was control transferred all at once?
- Did either party become more dependent after the first move?
- What enforcement mechanism existed when trust failed?
- Did short-term alignment conceal a predictable long-term conflict?

## Modern translation

The final answer should convert the mechanisms into modern design choices, for example:

- vesting;
- trial projects before equity commitments;
- explicit decision rights;
- IP ownership rules;
- information access boundaries;
- deadlock and exit provisions.

Historical analysis can reveal the mechanism; modern legal and governance advice must still rely on current professional sources.
